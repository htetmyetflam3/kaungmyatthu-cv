02 CSM · Retail systems

Finding the right part — not ordering it twice.

Shelf mapping + an in-store Point of Sale demo for an automotive-parts shop.

Overview

The problem the shelf map solved.

In a densely stocked parts shop, one shelf row can hold 30+ variants among 100+ pieces. Similar-looking parts fit different vehicles — so a missing item gets assumed out of stock, a replacement arrives, the original re-appears, and both keep scarce shelf space.

I designed the master shelf map — identify the code, find the position (MR-2 / SH-1 / R3), confirm the variant, replenish the same location — plus a Point of Sale demo for the floor. The map and the POS demo are the downloads below.

05 Prompt engineering

Prompts as a system — not a mind.

Self-authored manuscript on how prompts shape LLM responses — and how to read AI as a layered system.

Overview

The tech insight.

Most prompt-engineering guides say the job is writing perfect instructions. This manuscript — a self-authored book in three chapters — shows the opposite. An LLM responds as a layered system: the gate reads what you mean, and the premise, tone, safety and completion-control layers negotiate what gets said and how. A prompt changes the odds of a good answer — it does not guarantee it.

And the tech is moving fast. As the old template approach becomes mostly irrelevant, I work from the current picture: prompts are the input to a living system — I write with the layers in mind, not with canned templates, and stay honest about what a good prompt can and cannot do.

Self-authored manuscript LLM systems thinking Layers & zones Unfinished · personal exploration

Prompts as tasks

The conversation itself becomes the task. In LLM-based AI the user provides instructions, questions or examples and the system generates responses dynamically — in other AI types the task is embedded in control logic. Not all AI uses prompts; prompt-based interaction is what makes LLM-based AI fundamentally different.

Emergent behaviour, not magic

No single component decides. Just like a CPU, purposeful behaviour appears from the coordinated interaction of mathematical operations, learned parameters, sensory inputs and embedded rules — not an individual insight. Emergent behaviour results solely from the structured interaction of billions of parameters.

Layers & zones

Input layers decide whether a response is possible; adjustment layers decide how that response appears. Most “why did the AI respond like that?” moments trace back to this two-way split.

Ch. 1 Understanding AI as LLMs and Prompts — summary

Chapter 1 — Understanding AI as LLMs and Prompts

The book's first chapter sets the terms: AI is a broad term, but here it means text-based AI built on large language models. A prompt is a task delivered interactively — the user provides instructions, questions or examples, and the system generates responses dynamically.

The core idea: an AI does not reason like a person. Just like a CPU, no single component does the work alone — the behavior that looks intelligent emerges from the coordinated interaction of mathematical operations, learned parameters and rules. Emergent behavior, not magic.

  • Prompts are tasks — and only LLM-based AI takes prompts as interactive instructions.
  • Emergent behavior — the CPU analogy — explains why a prompt cannot be a "mind command."
  • A prompt as input — the next chapter names the layers that judge and shape the output.
Ch. 2 Prompt techniques — summary

Chapter 2 — Practical techniques (summary)

The book also covers the practical side — reusable skeletons, objective templates and ~11 techniques worth knowing, such as multitask / combined prompts, few-shot prompts (input-output examples to guide the AI) and negative prompts (what to avoid, not just what to do).

A reusable objective template helps define the purpose, tone, scope, tasks and constraints up front — before you write the prompt — so the final result is easier to check against that intent. The full chapter, examples and follow-up prompts are in the manuscript.

Chapter 3 — Layers & Zones: Why a Good Prompt Alone Isn’t Enough

If Chapter 2 taught you how to write clear prompts, this chapter shows why a good prompt is only part of the story. Even the clearest instructions must pass through the AI’s internal layers, each acting like an invisible moderator — shaping, nudging, or sometimes restraining the output.

Think of it this way: any system that learns from patterns will naturally develop patterns of its own. AI is no exception. Its training data and accumulated knowledge create predictable behaviors, and those behaviors interact with one another in ways that affect what you see.

Some of these layers are obvious — the Safety Layer, for example, is documented and verifiable in platform guidelines. Others aren’t spelled out anywhere, but their influence is real and observable. Even with perfect prompts, outputs can be vague, off‑topic, or overexplained. That’s not because the AI is broken — it’s because multiple internal forces are shaping the response simultaneously.

To the careful observer, these patterns are testable, but they may behave differently across AI versions. Advanced systems might slightly or obviously shift behavior, yet the effects remain present and meaningful, even if they disappear from immediate observation.

Punchy takeaway: the examples below are your playground — try them, notice the effects, and consider these zones while prompting. Doing so will put you closer to the expected result. Notice I said closer, not exact — that’s intentional, and that’s correct.

Throughout this chapter, layers refers to behavioral zones inside the AI that influence output, not the system infrastructure. What you’re reading is based on observation, reasoning, and pattern — not privileged knowledge — and yet, the influence is tangible.

Layers Explained: Input vs. Output

Throughout this chapter, the word layer refers to two related but different functions.To understand why a good prompt can still produce unexpected results, we need to separate:

  • Layers that evaluate the input
  • Layers that shape the output

This distinction explains most “why did the AI respond like that?” moments.

Input Layers — Interpretation & Evaluation
  • Layers without the word Adjustment operate on the input.

Their job is to understand what the user is asking before any response is generated.

They:

  • Interpret intent and assumptions
  • Evaluate context, constraints, and risk
  • Decide what is allowed, what is risky, and what is relevant

Examples:

  • Premise Layer → interprets assumptions and intent
  • Safety Layer → evaluates policy and risk boundaries
  • Context Layer → resolves scope, memory, and framing

These layers do not write the answer.They decide whether an answer can be given, and under what conditions.

Adjustment Layers — Response Shaping
  • Layers with the word Adjustment operate on the output.

Their job is not to judge the request, but to shape how the response is delivered.

They:

  • Modify tone, wording, and structure
  • Filter, soften, redirect, or reframe content
  • Apply constraints decided earlier by input layers

Examples:

  • Safety Adjustment Layer → rewrites or limits output after safety approval
  • Tone Adjustment Layer → modulates style, formality, or emotional distance
  • Knowledge Alignment Adjustment → selects an explanation strategy that fits the situation

These layers do not re-evaluate intent.They transform an already-approved response into a safer, clearer, or more appropriate form.

Why This Matters

A common confusion sounds like this:

  • “My prompt wasn’t unsafe — so why did the output feel restricted?”

Because both things can be true at once:

  • The Safety Layer approved the input
  • The Safety Adjustment Layer still reshaped the output to reduce risk

In simple terms:

  • Input layers decide if a response is possible
  • Adjustment layers decide how that response appears

If a layer judges the request, it operates on the input.If a layer reshapes the answer, it operates on the output.

Note

These layers are conceptual models, not claims about internal system architecture.They describe observable behavior, not proprietary implementation.

  • Next we start diving into the actual explaination of each layers.

1. The Gate: How AI Reads You First

Before generating any output, the AI passes your prompt through a preprocessing gate:

  • Intent: What are you really asking?
  • Goal: What do you want to accomplish?
  • Knowledge Level: How complex should the explanation be?

Emotional / Cognitive State: Should the tone be playful, serious, or neutral?

Think of this as the first moderator. If the AI misreads your intent here, even a perfect prompt can produce unexpected or vague results.

2. Core Layers: The Hidden Moderators

Large language models don’t just output text — they negotiate internally using multiple layers.

Premise Layer

The friendly moderator:

  • Treats the user’s stated assumptions as “true enough.”
  • Ensures reasoning is coherent and continuous.
  • Builds the logical path of the conversation based on your input.
Internal Reasoning Layer

Internal Reasoning Layer Role: This layer performs all the logical deductions, calculations, and planning behind the scenes. It doesn’t write the answer directly; instead, it decides what makes sense to say next based on your input, the AI’s accumulated knowledge, and context. Even though the internal reasoning layer doesn’t directly produce errors for good prompts, it can interact with other layers — like Safety, Tone, or User Knowledge — which sometimes causes the final output to feel incomplete, vague, or slightly off-topic. This is why a perfect prompt alone doesn’t always guarantee a perfect answer. Internal reasoning draws on patterns from training data and the AI’s accumulated knowledge. While not literally the same as the training data itself, for the purposes of shaping a response, they effectively act as a single knowledge source, guiding what the AI considers relevant or coherent. Clarification and edge cases:

In most situations, internal reasoning and accumulated knowledge work smoothly together. However, there are rare topics where the available patterns, sources, or representations are uneven or fragmented. In these cases, even a well‑structured prompt may receive an answer that feels vague, incomplete, or padded with general statements.

This doesn’t mean the prompt is wrong, and it doesn’t mean the AI failed. It simply reflects that no system has perfectly even coverage across all domains. Some subjects — especially niche, region‑specific, language‑locked, or culturally enclosed topics — provide less stable grounding for internal reasoning to build upon.

In those rare cases, internal reasoning still functions normally, but it has less solid material to work with, and when combined with other layers (such as User Knowledge or Safety), the final output may not fully satisfy the intent of the prompt.

This is not a flaw unique to ChatGPT or any single model. It’s a natural limitation of AI systems in general — and an expected tradeoff when working with probabilistic language models rather than curated databases.

Tone Layer – How AI Adjusts Style and Personality

The Tone Layer acts like a stylist or actor inside the AI. Its job is to shape how the response feels, independent of what the AI actually knows or concludes.

  • Adds style, empathy, humor, or formality depending on context.

Adjusts language to match expected personality or mood, such as playful, professional, or neutral.

Works alongside Premise and Safety, but cannot override safety boundaries.

Even wellcrafted prompts may produce a serious or neutral tone if tone isn’t explicitly specified. The AI often adjusts tone automatically based on content, so the Tone Layer’s effect is subtle but can still influence the perceived quality of the output.

Important: Even if a prompt requests “funny” content, the AI may produce a more neutral or serious tone if the instructions are ambiguous or the topic is sensitive. The Tone Layer tries to match the perceived intent, but it doesn’t always interpret it perfectly.

In short: Tone is layered, dynamic, and contextsensitive — but not guaranteed. It is influenced by content, safety, and perceived user intention.

Safety Layer

The serious moderator:

  • Monitors outputs for risk, harm, or sensitive content.
  • Overrides Premise or Tone when necessary.
  • Ensures style or reasoning never comes at the expense of safety.

3. Additional Layers

Premise Alignment Layer

Have you ever noticed that AI seems to “agree” with you more when you sound confident? Or that the same question, phrased slightly differently, produces very different responses? This isn’t a bug — it’s a behavior pattern called premise alignment.

  • Checks whether the user accepts the premise before elaborating.
  • If accepted, reasoning continues.
  • If resisted, the AI reframes or clarifies.

User Knowledge Alignment Layer

The User Knowledge Alignment Layer tailors explanations to what the AI estimates about the user’s familiarity with a topic. It does not judge — it adapts.

  • Example: Converting a JPG to a Word document
  • A response may suggest a simple online converter.

Another may suggest commandline or automated tools when technical familiarity is inferred.

The AI isn’t comparing users — it’s selecting an approach that seems most actionable.

4. Layer Interaction and Output Behavior

All layers work simultaneously, negotiating:

  • What gets said
  • How it’s said
  • What is omitted

Final Takeaways
  • Multiple moderators shape every response.
  • User stance changes output direction.
  • Understanding layers improves predictability.
  • Safety zones exist across platforms.
  • Word choice matters more than prompt length.
  • Alignment does not equal endorsement.

Completion Control Layer

Role:

This layer monitors the unfolding output as it’s being generated. Using patterns and heuristics, it evaluates whether the content — token by token — is moving toward satisfying the prompt. In the blockchain example from Chapter One, this is the layer that “checks” each section: whether part one fully explains what it is, part two covers how it works, and part three addresses why it matters.

It decides when to continue generating, pause, or move to the next section. Unlike Internal Reasoning, it doesn’t plan or add content itself — it observes, signals, and enforces limits based on what appears sufficient.

Observable effect for the user:

Sentences or sections may end naturally once this layer deems them complete.

In some cases, outputs may feel slightly abrupt or constrained, especially if other layers like Safety intervene simultaneously.

It ensures the AI doesn’t generate endlessly and keeps the response aligned with the intended structure of the prompt.

Key takeaway:

The Completion Control Layer is the “gatekeeper” of generation. It works silently behind the scenes, guiding when the AI should stop, pause, or proceed — ensuring the output reaches a coherent state without overstepping. This layer will be formally named and referenced in later chapters, but for now, think of it as the system that checks completeness in real time while the response is unfolding.

The central lesson

The manuscript isn’t about engineering better prompts — it’s about understanding AI as a system: the gate that reads you, the layers that negotiate what gets said, and why even a good prompt can be reshaped along the way.

That systemic lens — layers & zones — is the tech & AI skill this manuscript documents.
